Effective Notetaking

Notetaking is an essential skill for students and professionals. It helps you to retain and understand the information presented in various formats and media. By taking effective notes, you can identify the most important points, organize your thoughts, and prepare yourself for future recollection and application of important content.

Common Problems

Pick a problem. Find a method.

Some problems require practice and patience. Don’t confuse tool quality with your own level of refinement!

Addressing Challenges

Some of the issues we encounter while taking notes are hard to resolve. You can’t forcibly calm a rowdy environment or simplify an inherently complicated idea.

A number of common problems are best addressed with practice and patience. You can, for instance, work to expand your attention span over time by exercising restraint, eliminating distractions, and targeting your focus on more challenging media.

We can even resolve a few common quandaries by switching up our approach to notetaking! Different methods have their own strengths and weaknesses, and experimenting is an ideal way to determine what works best for you.

Active Listening

You’re in high school. You want to go to college. As academic content becomes more complex and students are preparing for higher education or the workforce, active listening is a foundational skill that supports academic success, social development, and the cultivation of essential skills for future endeavors.

Active listening is a communication skill that involves fully focusing, understanding, and responding to media in a thoughtful and engaged manner. It goes beyond simply reading or hearing words; it requires concentration, processing, and providing feedback through clarifying questions. This ensures a deeper understanding, engagement and retention of any topic.

Strategies for Active Listening

  • Pay Attention

    Ensure that you’re fully focused on a text, audio, video, or speaker.

  • Minimize Distractions

    Turn off apps, games, and social media. Put your phone away. Close irrelevant tabs.

  • Ask Questions

    Ask or record questions whenever you’re unsure or want to dig deeper.

  • Paraphrase Info

    Write down key information concisely. Use keywords and short phrases.

  • Review Notes

    As you learn more, revise and add details to what you’ve written.

  • Participate Actively

    Contribute to discussions. Sharing ideas improves comprehension and retention.

Common Methods

Ineffective notetaking often involves disorganized information, lack of visual cues, and incomplete details.

Effective notetaking methods enhance organization, comprehension, and retention of key information.

Let’s consider six popular and effective notetaking methods!

Cornell Method

Main Notes (Right Column):
Summarize key concepts and main ideas on the right side of the page.
Cues (Left Column):
Create cues or questions based on the main notes to aid in reviewing.
Summary (Bottom Section):
Write a summary at the bottom of the page to reinforce key takeaways.

Best For: Condensing and organizing complicated ideas and information while encouraging later review!

Outline Method

Outlines are composed of individual ideas identified by bullet points.

They summarize an article’s logic, with main topics supported by subtopics and details.

Outlines are hierarchical: they make it clear what aspects of a topic are most important.

Outlining proves tricky if you struggle to identify main ideas.

Best For: Establishing hierarchy and breaking ideas down into key components!

Map Method

Central Topic
Place the main concept or topic in the center of the page.
Branches
Create branches for subtopics, using keywords and brief phrases.
Color & Images
Use colors and simple images to enhance visual memory.

Best For: Demonstrating relationships and causality! Integrating doodles!

Chart Method

Before class, work to identify important categories and topics. What are the main topics and subtopics? What categories of information do your subtopics fall into?

Set up your paper or spreadsheet before class. Create columns that represent each category and rows that represent each subtopic. Add an extra column on the far right side of your chart for unanticipated information.

During class, add words, phrases, main ideas, descriptions, and more about each subtopic in the appropriate category column.

Best For: Preparing before class; noticing unexpected relationships between ideas!

Sentence Method

Sentencing represents ideas or concepts in individual sentences.

If you struggle with outlining, sentencing can help you sort main and supporting ideas.

As you read or listen, record whatever seems important using a full sentence.

When you’re done, reorder your sentences to emulate (or reorganize!) the author’s argument.

Best For: Sorting disorganized information; people who aren’t sure where to start!

Sketch Method

A visual notetaking technique that combines drawing, text, and symbols to capture ideas, concepts, and information.

Approaches vary depending on your temperament and subject matter.

During a meeting or discussion, you may aim to capture key points, action items, and decisions.

During school hours, you can summarize lectures, readings, and class materials.

You can generate and visualize new ideas while brainstorming; you might also sketch to track goals, habits, and progress.

The Basics

Take it easy! Grab a blank page and a few pens, crayons, markers, or pencils.

Begin each page with basic shapes, lines, and short snippets of text. Layer in more detail as you work. Try to include:

  • Icons: Simple, recognizable symbols that represent ideas or concepts.
  • Connectors: Lines, arrows, and other shapes that show relationships.
  • Text: Short, concise phrases or keywords that provide context.
  • Color: Used to highlight key points or differentiate between ideas.

Tips and Tricks

  • Keep It Simple: Avoid clutter and excessive detail.
  • Use Color Strategically: Highlight key points and differentiate between ideas.
  • Label Everything: Add text to clarify meaning.
  • Practice: Sketchnote regularly to improve your skills and develop your own style. Strive for a consistent style, especially when using abbreviations, icons, and connectors.
  • Embrace White Space: Let your work breathe. Use white space to emphasize important elements or create visual hierarchy.

Best For: Low-stakes notetaking; improving retention, understanding, and engagement.
